03What I built

Ten releases, one diff, and a rule written before the first query.

USDA publishes a database of the packaged foods manufacturers have submitted, by barcode, with the net weight the manufacturer declared. A fresh copy comes out about twice a year. Line the copies up by barcode and a product that got lighter shows up as two rows.

04Kept honest

The first count was 210. The audit cut it to a range.

The build's first gate, on a three-release slice, counted 210 shrink events. Many were one change copied into later releases; they collapse to 147. On the full ledger, two agent raters then read 97 events against the raw rows. About two in three were real downsizes. The rest were rounding, unit mix-ups, typos and barcodes reused for a different product.

  1. The event rule was written down and committed before any diff ran. A change after that is logged with its reason.
  2. Every count says "at least". Every rate carries its n and interval.
  3. The numbers in the docs are filled from one summary file. None is typed by hand, and this page is built the same way.
  4. A weight that drops and comes back, or changes with the product's name or recipe, never counts.
  5. When a fix changes the events, an audit label carries to the new build only if its event is the same two rows at the same tier. A Lindt bar whose weight string lost its gram figure read as a shrink until the rule learned to compare in a unit both rows print; its label stayed behind.
05The miss

It found none of the ten shrinks in the news.

I listed 10 downsizes the press reported, from Gatorade to Wheat Thins, and checked the ledger against them. It found 0 of 10 (95% interval 0% to 28%).

Reported downsizeSizesWhat the USDA data shows

In 7 of the 10, both sizes are in the data under two barcodes: the big pack on one, the small pack on another, and no barcode carries both. A brand that shrinks a product usually gives the smaller pack a new barcode. The old row stays in the database as it was. A diff that follows a barcode never sees a change.

06Second pass

So I followed the product's name instead of its barcode.

The second detector groups barcodes by brand, category and product name. A step is a new barcode that first shows up in a later release, 2% to 20% lighter than the nearest size in its family, where that size is new to the family and the old size stops getting new barcodes.

A new size is often just a new size, so the rule also runs backwards: new barcodes that came in heavier, over the same width on a log scale. That count is the yardstick. Lighter beats heavier 1.6 to 1.

Tier A: same ingredient listTier B: list changed

Then the same kind of audit: 100 sampled steps, each with both raw rows and the family's whole size history, read by two agent raters.

About one in four held up as one pack replacing another. The rest were mostly two sizes sold side by side, or two products that share a name. So the range is wide: at least 22, and up to 128.

It still finds none of the 10 press cases at their sizes. Gatorade shows why: its 32.0 and 28.0 fl oz bottles are both in the first release, on different barcodes, under one name. That switch happened before the data starts.

07What I learned

Six things the data taught me.

01

The barcode is not the product

02

The database mostly stopped moving in 2023

In the newest release, 97% of barcodes were last published before 2024, and 318 of the 348 new-barcode steps arrive in the 2022 and 2023 releases. USDA's own record pages say why: Label Insight, the partner behind 97% of these rows, stopped sending updates on November 16, 2023. "Since 2021" here means 2021 to 2023.

03

A first count is a guess

The first gate counted 210 shrink events, or 147 changes once copies were folded. The full ledger had 101 barcodes before the audit and 39 to 85 after it. Had I posted the first number at midnight, it would have been more than twice the top of the range I can defend.

04

The government row can be wrong

Claude read the net weight off 219 Open Food Facts label photos of products whose weight never changed. It agreed with the USDA row 80% of the time (75% to 85%). The smaller Sonnet got 79% from the same photos and 45% (38% to 51%) when told only the product's name. Checked against the photos, its reads held up on 98.9% (n=178). In one, an oven-roasted turkey breast, the photo showed the USDA row was the wrong one: every release says 9 oz/255 g; the tub says 8 oz.
